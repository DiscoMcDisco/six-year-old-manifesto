# Public Handbook direction

## Design intent

The Six-Year-Old Manifesto should feel like a calm public handbook: welcoming, practical, curious and honest. The child is the protagonist. Technology is one of the tools around them.

## Principles

1. Invite rather than impress.
2. Use ordinary language before specialist language.
3. Keep evidence visible and uncertainty explicit.
4. Use warm paper, ink and restrained functional colour.
5. Avoid technological theatre, corporate jargon and claims larger than the evidence.
6. Never attach the imagined child to a country unless geography is materially relevant to evidence.

## Public language

- Measured Enabling Substrate: What the world makes possible
- Institutional bridging: Help beyond the answer
- Practical pathway quality: Could a child actually do this?
- Agency and participation: Can people take part and shape decisions?
- Known unknowns: What we still cannot measure
- Evidence confidence: How sure are we?

Technical identifiers remain unchanged in data and methodology.

## Colour roles

- Paper: background and calm reading space
- Charcoal: primary text and serious calls to action
- Blue: evidence and explanation
- Green: progress and available routes
- Amber: caution and uncertainty
- Muted red: failure or serious concern

## Design governance extension

The Public Handbook direction remains the canonical guide to public tone, language and reading experience.

The later [Design Constitution](design-constitution.md) establishes the enduring rules behind the interface. The [Semantic Evidence Language](semantic-evidence-language.md) now provides the more precise governing definition for colour use.

Where the broad colour roles above could be read as decorative or brand-oriented, SEL takes precedence:

- blue means evidenced
- green means achievable now
- amber means plausible or emerging
- red means unsupported, blocked or contradicted
- grey means unknown

Paper, charcoal and neutral surfaces remain the reading environment rather than evidence states. Implementation changes should extend the Public Handbook treatment without erasing its existing history or language decisions.
